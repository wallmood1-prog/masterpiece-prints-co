# The needle in the eye

[visual: candle light old room]
In the year 1666, a young man in the English countryside picked up a long, blunt needle. The kind of needle a tailor might use to thread a cord through fabric. He sat down, steadied his hand, and slowly pushed it into the corner of his own eye. Not into the eyeball itself, but around it. Between the eye and the bone of the skull. As far back as he could go.

[visual: old handwritten notebook]
And then, calmly, he pressed. He wanted to change the shape of his eye from the outside, and watch what happened to his vision. He wrote it all down in his notebook. He saw circles of white, dark and coloured light, and they grew stronger the harder he pressed.

[visual: prism light rainbow]
That young man was Isaac Newton. The same Isaac Newton who would go on to explain gravity, invent much of calculus and split white light into a rainbow with a glass prism. He was trying to understand how light and colour actually work. And when he needed an experimental subject, the most convenient one was sitting right there in his chair.

[visual: laboratory dark dramatic]
Newton was lucky. He didn't go blind. But the history of science is full of people who did exactly what he did: they decided that the fastest way to the truth was through their own body. Some of them won Nobel Prizes. Some of them were mocked for the rest of their lives. And some of them never came home.

[visual: microscope scientist]
Today we're going to meet them. The surgeon who pushed a tube into his own heart. The doctor who drank a glass of bacteria to prove the entire medical world wrong. The man who was stopped from over a thousand kilometres per hour in less than a second and a half, on purpose. And the questions their stories still raise, because every one of them forces us to ask: how far should you go to prove you're right?

# The tube in the heart

[visual: old hospital corridor]
Let's start in Germany, in the summer of 1929. In a small hospital in the town of Eberswalde, north of Berlin, works a 25-year-old surgical resident named Werner Forssmann. He is junior, unknown, and he has an idea that his superiors consider completely insane.

[visual: human heart anatomy]
At the time, the human heart was a no-go zone. Doctors could listen to it, they could feel its rhythm at the wrist, but nobody dared put anything inside a living, beating heart. The general belief was simple: touch the inside of the heart, and it stops.

[visual: medical tube catheter]
Forssmann thought otherwise. He had read about experiments on horses in the 1800s, where researchers had passed tubes through the veins into the heart. He believed you could do the same thing in a human being: slide a thin, flexible tube into a vein in the arm and push it, gently, all the way to the heart. If it worked, doctors could one day deliver medicine directly into the heart, or measure what was happening inside it.

[visual: doctor refusing]
He asked his boss for permission to try it on a patient. The answer was no. He asked if he could try it on himself. The answer was, again, no.

[visual: hospital operating room vintage]
So Forssmann did it anyway. He needed sterile instruments, and those were controlled by the operating room nurse, Gerda Ditzen. He told her about his plan. Ditzen was so convinced by the idea that she volunteered to be the test subject herself. Forssmann agreed, and strapped her to the operating table. And then, while she was strapped down, he turned around and did the procedure on his own arm instead.

[visual: arm vein close up]
He numbed his elbow, made a small cut, found a vein, and began to feed in a thin rubber tube, the kind normally used in urology. Centimetre after centimetre. Into the vein, up the arm, toward the shoulder, and then down into the chest.

[visual: stairs old building]
Then came the part that makes this story legendary. To prove where the tube actually was, he needed an X-ray. And the X-ray machine was in the basement. So Werner Forssmann, with a rubber tube running up his arm and into his chest, walked down the stairs to the X-ray room.

[visual: x-ray chest image]
There, the image showed it clearly. The tip of the tube had reached the right side of his heart. He was alive. His heart was beating normally. The impossible had just been done, by a junior doctor, on himself, against orders.

[visual: newspaper vintage]
You might expect a hero's welcome. Instead, Forssmann was reprimanded. The medical establishment called it a circus trick. He lost his position, and his career in cardiology was essentially over before it began. He eventually became a urologist, far from the field he had just transformed.

[visual: hospital cardiac monitor]
But other doctors read his paper. In the United States, two researchers, André Cournand and Dickinson Richards, took his technique and turned it into a real diagnostic tool: cardiac catheterisation. Today it is used millions of times every year, to diagnose heart disease, to open blocked arteries, to place stents. If you know someone who has had a stent, you know someone whose life was touched by that walk down the stairs.

[visual: award ceremony medal]
In 1956, Forssmann received a phone call. He, Cournand and Richards had been awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The man who had been thrown out for his experiment was now being honoured for it. He reportedly said that he felt like a village parson who had just been told he'd been made a bishop.

# The man who drank the bacteria

[visual: perth australia city]
Let's jump forward fifty-five years, and to the other side of the world. Perth, Western Australia, 1984. A young doctor named Barry Marshall is frustrated.

[visual: stomach pain person]
For decades, doctors had been absolutely certain about one thing: stomach ulcers were caused by stress, spicy food and too much acid. The treatment was antacids, a bland diet, and in serious cases, surgery to remove part of the stomach. Ulcers came back again and again. Millions of people lived with them for life.

[visual: pathologist microscope slides]
But Marshall's colleague, the pathologist Robin Warren, had noticed something strange. In the stomach tissue of patients with gastritis and ulcers, he kept finding a curved, spiral-shaped bacterium. Everyone had been taught that bacteria could not survive in the stomach. It's simply too acidic. So most experts assumed these bacteria were a contamination, or irrelevant.

[visual: bacteria animation]
Marshall and Warren believed the opposite. They believed this bacterium, later named Helicobacter pylori, was the true cause of most ulcers. If they were right, ulcers weren't a lifestyle disease. They were an infection. And infections can be cured with antibiotics.

[visual: scientist presentation audience]
The medical world was not impressed. Their research was rejected and criticised. Marshall tried to prove the link in animals, infecting piglets, but it didn't work. He was stuck. He needed proof that the bacterium could make a healthy stomach sick.

[visual: glass of cloudy liquid]
So, one day in 1984, Barry Marshall had his own stomach checked to confirm it was healthy. And then he took a culture of Helicobacter pylori, mixed it into a broth, and drank it.

[visual: man sick bed]
For a few days, nothing happened. Then the symptoms began. Nausea. Vomiting. Bad breath. When his stomach was examined again, about ten days later, the tissue showed exactly what Marshall had predicted: inflammation, and the spiral bacteria thriving in it. He had given himself gastritis, the condition that comes before an ulcer. He then treated himself with antibiotics and recovered.

[visual: medical journal pages]
His self-experiment didn't convince everyone overnight. Changing medical dogma rarely happens in a day. But it was a turning point. Over the following years, more studies confirmed the link, and treatment changed around the world. Ulcers that once meant a lifetime of pain could now be cured with a course of antibiotics.

[visual: nobel prize stockholm]
In 2005, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. And the image of a doctor drinking a glass of bacteria to prove a point became one of the most famous moments in modern medicine.

# The professor who drank cholera

[visual: old european city 19th century]
But here's the thing. Drinking a dangerous germ to win an argument doesn't always mean you're right. To understand that, we have to go back to Munich, in 1892.

[visual: old scientist portrait]
Max von Pettenkofer was one of the most respected scientists in Germany. He was a pioneer of public hygiene. He had pushed for clean water and better sewers in Munich, and he was right that these things save lives. But he had a very particular theory about cholera.

[visual: dirty water river]
Cholera was terrifying. A disease that could kill a healthy adult within a day, through extreme diarrhoea and dehydration. In 1892, a cholera epidemic was tearing through the city of Hamburg, killing thousands of people.

[visual: laboratory petri dish]
A few years earlier, the German scientist Robert Koch had identified a bacterium, Vibrio cholerae, as the cause of the disease. Pettenkofer didn't accept that. He believed the bacterium alone wasn't enough. In his view, it needed specific conditions in the soil and groundwater to become dangerous. The germ, he thought, was only part of the story.

[visual: old man drinking glass]
So, at the age of seventy-four, Pettenkofer obtained a culture of cholera bacteria and drank it in front of witnesses. According to accounts of the event, he even took bicarbonate of soda beforehand to neutralise his stomach acid, so the bacteria would have every chance to survive.

[visual: old bed sick man]
And what happened? He developed diarrhoea, but a mild case. He survived. Some of his students repeated the experiment, and also survived. Pettenkofer declared victory.

[visual: rain window sad]
But he was wrong. Today we know that Koch was right: Vibrio cholerae causes cholera. Pettenkofer probably survived because of a combination of factors. The culture may have weakened in the lab, he may have had some immunity, and plenty of people infected with cholera only get a mild illness. A single person surviving proves very little.

[visual: question mark concept]
And this is the first big lesson of self-experimentation. A sample of one is not proof. Marshall's experiment was powerful because it matched a prediction and was later confirmed by large studies. Pettenkofer's was dramatic, brave, and scientifically meaningless.

# Drinking the vomit

[visual: philadelphia old street]
If you think drinking cholera is extreme, let's go back even further, to Philadelphia at the beginning of the 1800s. The city had suffered terrible outbreaks of yellow fever, a disease that causes high fever, jaundice, which turns the skin yellow, internal bleeding and a characteristic black vomit.

[visual: young student books candle]
A medical student named Stubbins Ffirth was convinced that yellow fever was not contagious. He wanted to prove that you couldn't catch it from a sick person. And so he began a series of experiments that are, frankly, hard to describe over dinner.

[visual: scalpel medical vintage]
He made small cuts in his arm and poured into them the black vomit collected from yellow fever patients. He dripped it into his eyes. He breathed in the fumes. And eventually, he drank it. He also exposed himself to other fluids from patients.

[visual: man walking alone street]
He never got sick. In 1804, he published his findings, concluding that yellow fever was not contagious.

[visual: mosquito close up]
And in a way, he was right, but for the wrong reason. Yellow fever doesn't spread directly from person to person. It's spread by mosquitoes. And the fluids Ffirth used probably came from patients in the late stage of the disease, when the virus is no longer easily transmitted. He did all of that, and the real answer was buzzing around his head the entire time.

# The bite in Cuba

[visual: tropical island havana]
The mosquito answer would cost another man his life. In 1900, the United States Army sent a team of doctors to Cuba, led by Walter Reed, to find out once and for all how yellow fever spread. Soldiers were dying of the disease in large numbers.

[visual: mosquito on skin]
A Cuban doctor, Carlos Finlay, had proposed decades earlier that a specific mosquito transmitted yellow fever. Few people took him seriously. The American team decided to test his idea, and some of them chose to test it on themselves.

[visual: young doctor portrait]
One of them was Jesse Lazear, a 34-year-old doctor with a wife and two young children back home. In September 1900, Lazear was bitten by an infected mosquito. Officially, the bite was recorded as an accident, but historians have long suspected that it was deliberate. His notebooks suggest he was testing the theory on himself.

[visual: hospital bed old]
Within days he developed yellow fever. On the 25th of September, 1900, Jesse Lazear died.

[visual: cemetery quiet]
His death, and the controlled experiments that followed, confirmed that mosquitoes spread yellow fever. That knowledge led to mosquito control campaigns that wiped the disease out of Havana and made it possible to build the Panama Canal. Lazear never saw any of it. He's one of the reasons self-experimentation is not a fun story. It's a real gamble, and sometimes the house wins.

# The fastest man on Earth

[visual: desert new mexico]
Not every self-experimenter was a doctor in a lab. Some of them were strapped to rockets.

[visual: jet aircraft vintage]
After the Second World War, aircraft were becoming faster than ever. And pilots had a terrifying question: if they had to eject, or if their plane crashed, how much deceleration could the human body survive? At the time, many experts believed that anything beyond about 18 g, eighteen times the force of gravity, would kill a person.

[visual: military officer portrait]
An American Air Force doctor named John Paul Stapp decided to find out. Not with dummies. With himself.

[visual: rocket launch smoke]
Stapp rode a series of rocket-powered sleds on rails. The sleds were accelerated to enormous speeds and then stopped violently by water brakes. Ride after ride, he pushed the limits further. He broke his wrists. He cracked ribs. He lost fillings from his teeth.

[visual: rocket sled speed]
On the 10th of December, 1954, at Holloman Air Force Base in New Mexico, Stapp climbed onto a sled called Sonic Wind Number 1. Nine rockets fired behind him. In five seconds, he reached a speed of about 1,017 kilometres per hour, around 632 miles per hour. Faster than a bullet from a typical handgun.

[visual: crash impact slow motion]
And then the sled hit the water brakes. In about 1.4 seconds, he went from over a thousand kilometres per hour to a complete stop. His body experienced a peak force of more than 46 g. For a moment, he effectively weighed over forty-six times his normal weight.

[visual: eye close up red]
The blood vessels in his eyes burst. For a while, he couldn't see. But his vision came back, and he walked away. He had proven that a properly restrained human body could survive forces far beyond what anyone believed.

[visual: car seat belt]
The impact of that day is all around you. Stapp became a passionate advocate for seatbelts in cars, at a time when most cars didn't have them. His research helped shape safety standards for aircraft and automobiles. Every time you click your seatbelt, you're using knowledge that was paid for with the eyes and bones of John Paul Stapp.

[visual: engineer blueprint]
And here's a bonus fact. Stapp's team is connected to one of the most famous sayings in the world. During earlier deceleration tests, an engineer named Edward Murphy was involved in a sensor installation that went wrong. The phrase that came out of that project, popularised by Stapp himself at a press conference, was: "Anything that can go wrong, will go wrong." Murphy's Law.

# The anaesthesia test

[visual: old surgery theatre]
Let's go back to medicine, and to a story that is almost comic, if it weren't so painful. Germany, 1898. The surgeon August Bier was experimenting with a new idea: injecting cocaine, then used as a local anaesthetic, into the fluid around the spinal cord. If it worked, you could operate on the lower body of a patient who was fully awake, but felt nothing.

[visual: syringe medical]
Bier decided to test it on himself, with the help of his assistant, August Hildebrandt. Hildebrandt tried to give Bier the injection first, but it went wrong. Spinal fluid leaked out, the anaesthetic was lost, and the test failed.

[visual: two doctors talking]
So they swapped places. Bier injected Hildebrandt. And this time, it worked. To test how complete the numbness was, Bier did a series of things that would be considered extremely creative today. He pricked Hildebrandt's leg with a needle. He pressed a burning cigar against his skin. He hit his shin with a hammer. Hildebrandt felt almost nothing.

[visual: men celebrating wine dinner]
The two of them celebrated that evening with dinner and wine. The next day, they both woke up with terrible headaches, a side effect we now know is caused by spinal fluid leaking from the puncture. Hildebrandt also had very bruised legs, which is not surprising. But spinal anaesthesia had been born, and versions of it are still used today, for example in many caesarean sections.

# The nerve that was cut on purpose

[visual: hand nerves anatomy]
One more story, and it's one of the most patient experiments ever done. In 1903, the British neurologist Henry Head wanted to understand how our sense of touch, pain and temperature works. And how it comes back after a nerve is damaged.

[visual: surgeon hands]
Patients with nerve injuries were not ideal subjects. They were often in shock, in pain, and not trained to describe exactly what they felt. So Head asked a surgeon colleague to cut a nerve in his own forearm, and then sew the ends back together.

[visual: man writing notes table]
For roughly the next four years, Head and his collaborator, the psychologist W.H.R. Rivers, mapped how sensation slowly returned to his hand and arm. They tested it with pins, with hot and cold objects, with cotton wool, with hairs. Week after week. They were looking for patterns in how the nervous system rebuilds itself. Some of their conclusions were later revised, but the experiment shaped decades of neurology.

# Why they did it

[visual: thinking man silhouette]
So, why? Why would intelligent, educated people do this to themselves?

[visual: scientist looking at stars]
There are a few reasons, and they're not all noble. Some of them were genuinely ethical. They felt that if an experiment was too risky for them, it was too risky for anyone else. Asking a patient to take a risk you wouldn't take yourself felt wrong.

[visual: closed door]
Some of them had no choice. Their ideas were rejected, they couldn't get permission, and doing it on themselves was the only path left. Forssmann and Marshall both fall into this category.

[visual: man alone thinking]
And some of them were driven by something more human: pride, stubbornness, the need to be right. Pettenkofer drank cholera not to discover a truth, but to defend an idea he refused to give up.

[visual: modern laboratory clean]
Today, self-experimentation is rare and heavily discouraged. Modern research has ethics committees, informed consent, safety reviews and controlled trials with thousands of participants. These rules exist for a reason. Some of them were written after real abuses, where people were experimented on without their knowledge or consent. Compared to those, the self-experimenters were, in a strange way, among the most honest: the only person they were willing to risk was themselves.

[visual: crowd people walking]
But the modern system also exists because a single brave act is not enough. Marshall needed large studies after his glass of bacteria. Forssmann needed Cournand and Richards to turn his trick into medicine. Science is not one hero taking a risk. It's many people, checking, doubting and confirming.

# What we owe them

[visual: hospital modern heart surgery]
And yet, it's hard not to feel a kind of respect. Every time a cardiologist threads a catheter into a patient's heart, there's a little bit of a young German resident walking down a staircase. Every time someone's ulcer is cured with antibiotics, there's a little bit of a doctor in Perth drinking a glass of bacteria. Every time you buckle a seatbelt, there's a little bit of John Paul Stapp, with bloodshot eyes, walking away from a rocket sled.

[visual: sunset horizon calm]
And there's also Jesse Lazear, who didn't walk away. Science advances on knowledge. But sometimes, that knowledge has a name, and a face, and a family waiting at home.
