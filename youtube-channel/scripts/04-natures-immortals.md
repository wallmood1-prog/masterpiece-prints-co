# The jellyfish that goes back in time

[visual: jellyfish underwater]
Imagine you are old. Your body is tired, your cells are worn out, and you're close to the end. And then, instead of dying, you simply go back to being a baby. You start your life again. And when you get old a second time, you do it again. And again. And again.

[visual: tiny jellyfish macro]
For us, this is science fiction. For a tiny jellyfish, smaller than your fingernail, it's real. And it's not the only creature on Earth that seems to have found a loophole in the most universal rule of life: that everything that lives, must die.

[visual: ocean deep blue]
Today we're going to meet nature's immortals. A shark that may have been swimming when Galileo was alive. A microscopic animal that survived being exposed to outer space. Worms that came back to life after tens of thousands of years frozen in the ground. A rodent that seems to ignore aging. And we'll ask the question everyone wants answered: what can they teach us about our own lives, and our own deaths?

# Turritopsis

[visual: italian coast riviera]
Our story starts in Italy. In the late 1980s, a German marine biology student named Christian Sommer was collecting small marine animals along the coast near Rapallo, on the Italian Riviera. Among them was a tiny jellyfish, today known as Turritopsis dohrnii. Adults are only about four or five millimetres across.

[visual: aquarium lab tank]
Sommer kept them in the lab to study their life cycle. And he noticed something impossible. The jellyfish didn't seem to die. Instead, some of them seemed to disappear, and in their place there were small blobs that eventually developed into polyps, the early stage of a jellyfish's life.

[visual: jellyfish life cycle]
To understand why this is so strange, we need to know how a jellyfish normally lives. Most jellyfish start as a larva, which settles on a surface and becomes a polyp, a small, plant-like stalk attached to a rock. The polyp then produces young jellyfish, called medusae, which swim away, grow up, reproduce, and die. It's a one-way street, like our own life: baby, adult, death.

[visual: transformation morph]
Turritopsis can drive that street in reverse. When it's stressed, injured, starving or simply aging, the adult jellyfish can sink, shrink, absorb its tentacles and transform back into a polyp. Its cells change identity, a process called transdifferentiation. Cells that were part of the adult jellyfish turn into different types of cells needed for the young polyp. And from that new polyp, new jellyfish bud off, genetically identical to the original.

[visual: jellyfish glowing dark]
In theory, this cycle can repeat indefinitely. That's why Turritopsis dohrnii is often called the immortal jellyfish. But "immortal" needs an asterisk. These jellyfish can still be eaten by predators. They can still be killed by disease. In the wild, most of them probably die long before they get a chance to rejuvenate. What they've escaped is not death. It's aging. They are biologically immortal, but not invincible.

[visual: dna strands animation]
In 2022, a team of Spanish researchers sequenced the genome of Turritopsis dohrnii and compared it with a close relative that cannot rejuvenate. They found differences in genes linked to DNA repair, to the protection of telomeres, the caps at the ends of our chromosomes, and to the way cells are reprogrammed. The secret of the immortal jellyfish is not one magic gene. It's a whole toolkit.

# The water bear

[visual: microscope moss sample]
Let's shrink down even further. Take a clump of moss from a wall or a tree, put it in a bit of water, and look at it under a microscope. There's a good chance you'll see something wonderful: a tiny, chubby animal with eight stubby legs, little claws, and a clumsy, almost cute way of walking.

[visual: microscopic creature]
This is a tardigrade, also known as a water bear or a moss piglet. Most of them are less than a millimetre long. They were first described in 1773 by a German pastor, Johann August Ephraim Goeze, who called them little water bears. They live almost everywhere: in moss, in soil, in oceans, on mountains, in Antarctic ice.

[visual: dry cracked earth]
And they have a superpower. When their environment dries out, they don't die. They pull in their legs and head, curl up into a tiny dry ball called a tun, and lose almost all the water in their body. Their metabolism slows down to an almost undetectable level. They are, essentially, paused.

[visual: water drop falling]
In this state, they can stay dormant for years. And when water returns, they rehydrate, uncurl, and walk away as if nothing had happened.

[visual: space station orbit]
In 2007, scientists sent dried tardigrades into space aboard a European Space Agency mission called FOTON-M3. They were exposed directly to the vacuum of space, and some to the Sun's harsh ultraviolet radiation, for about ten days. When they returned to Earth and were rehydrated, many of the tardigrades exposed to the vacuum survived, and some even went on to reproduce. They were the first animals known to survive direct exposure to open space.

[visual: x ray radiation]
Some tardigrade species can also tolerate doses of radiation hundreds of times higher than what would kill a human. In 2016, Japanese scientists discovered a protein in one tardigrade species, which they named Dsup, for "damage suppressor". It seems to wrap around DNA and shield it. When they inserted the gene for this protein into human cells in the lab, those cells suffered noticeably less DNA damage from X-rays.

[visual: moon surface]
And here's a strange footnote. In 2019, an Israeli lunar lander called Beresheet crashed on the Moon. It was carrying a small archive of human knowledge, and, as a private addition to the payload, thousands of dried tardigrades. Scientists doubt they survived the impact, but somewhere on the Moon, there may be the remains of the most resilient animals on Earth.

[visual: hot sun desert]
But tardigrades are not truly indestructible. When they're active and hydrated, they're actually quite fragile. A 2020 study found that active tardigrades exposed to temperatures around 37 degrees Celsius for a day began to die in large numbers. Their superpowers mostly work when they're dried out and paused. Their real trick is not being invincible. It's knowing how to wait.

# The four-hundred-year-old shark

[visual: arctic ocean ice]
From the tiniest animals, let's go to one of the largest fish in the Arctic. In the cold, dark waters of the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, there lives a shark that can grow to more than five metres long. It swims incredibly slowly, often less than one kilometre per hour. It's called the Greenland shark.

[visual: shark slow swimming]
For a long time, nobody knew how long these sharks lived. Fish are often aged by counting rings in hard parts of the body, a bit like tree rings. But Greenland sharks don't have the right kind of hard structures. Their age was a mystery.

[visual: eye close up animal]
In 2016, a team led by the Danish scientist Julius Nielsen found a clever solution. The lens of the eye contains proteins that are formed before birth and never replaced. So the scientists used radiocarbon dating on the centre of the eye lenses of twenty-eight sharks.

[visual: atomic bomb test historic]
They also used a historic marker. Nuclear bomb tests in the 1950s and 1960s released large amounts of carbon-14 into the atmosphere, which then entered the ocean food chain. Sharks born after that period had a "bomb pulse" signature in their eyes. Sharks born before it didn't. That helped calibrate the method.

[visual: old map ship sailing]
The results were astonishing. The largest shark in the study, a female just over five metres long, was estimated to be around 392 years old. The uncertainty is large, plus or minus 120 years, so she could have been as young as 272, or as old as about 512. Even at the lower end, the Greenland shark is the longest-lived vertebrate known to science.

[visual: telescope old astronomy]
Think about it. If the central estimate is right, that shark was born in the early 1600s. Around the time Galileo was pointing his telescope at the sky. She swam through the cold ocean while empires rose and fell, while steam engines were invented, while humans first flew, through two world wars and into the age of the internet.

[visual: cold water underwater]
The same study suggested that Greenland sharks don't reach sexual maturity until they're around 150 years old. Their secret is probably related to their slow pace of life: cold water, slow metabolism, slow growth, slow everything. But a genome analysis published as a preprint in 2024 suggested there may be more to it, including many copies of genes linked to DNA repair. Once again, a long life seems to require excellent maintenance.

# Back from the permafrost

[visual: siberia permafrost frozen]
Now let's travel to Siberia. Here, the ground is permanently frozen. It's called permafrost, and in some places it has been frozen for tens of thousands of years. It's a natural freezer, and scientists have found that it contains more than mammoth bones.

[visual: microscopic organism water]
In 2021, Russian scientists reported that they had revived bdelloid rotifers, tiny multicellular animals that live in water, from permafrost samples around 24,000 years old. After they thawed, the rotifers started moving, feeding and reproducing. Rotifers reproduce without males, by cloning themselves, so the frozen ancestors started new populations.

[visual: worm nematode microscope]
Two years later, in 2023, another study reported something even more astonishing. Nematodes, microscopic roundworms, were revived from Siberian permafrost dated to around 46,000 years ago. That's a time when Neanderthals still lived in Europe, and woolly mammoths roamed the steppes. The worms belonged to a species previously unknown to science, now named Panagrolaimus kolymaensis. After they were revived in the lab, they reproduced, generation after generation.

[visual: ocean floor deep]
And microbes go even further. In 2020, a team from the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology reported reviving microbes from sediment under the floor of the South Pacific Ocean, where they had been buried for over 100 million years in an environment with almost no food. Given nutrients in the lab, many of them woke up and began to grow and divide.

[visual: frozen ice crystals]
These organisms don't defeat death by staying young. They defeat time by stopping it. Their life is put on pause, and when conditions are right again, it resumes.

# The frog that freezes solid

[visual: frog forest]
We don't have to go to Siberia to see this kind of pause. In the forests of North America lives a small brown frog called the wood frog. And every winter, it does something that would kill almost any other animal: it freezes.

[visual: frozen forest snow]
As the temperature drops, ice crystals form in its body. Up to about two thirds of the water in its body can turn to ice. Its heart stops beating. Its breathing stops. There's no measurable brain activity. If you found one, you'd think it was dead.

[visual: sugar crystals macro]
The frog survives because its liver floods its cells with glucose, a sugar, which acts as a natural antifreeze. The sugar keeps the inside of the cells from freezing and being torn apart by ice crystals. The ice forms mostly outside the cells, where it does less damage.

[visual: spring forest sunlight]
And in spring, the frog thaws. Within hours, its heart starts beating again, it starts breathing, and it hops away. Researchers who study organ preservation are very interested in tricks like this. Today, donated human organs can only be kept for a limited time. Learning how to freeze and thaw tissues safely could one day change transplant medicine.

# The rodent that ignores aging

[visual: underground tunnel]
Now let's meet an animal that has been called ugly, but might be one of the most important animals in aging research: the naked mole-rat.

[visual: small rodent close up]
Naked mole-rats live underground in East Africa, in colonies organised a bit like those of ants or bees, with a single breeding queen. They're about the size of a mouse, almost hairless, with wrinkly pink skin and big front teeth.

[visual: mouse laboratory]
A mouse of that size typically lives two or three years. Naked mole-rats can live more than thirty years. Records show individuals living to around 37. That's like a human living for hundreds of years.

[visual: graph chart data]
But the most surprising thing is not just how long they live. It's how they age, or rather, how they don't. In almost every animal, the risk of dying increases as you get older. For humans, the risk of death roughly doubles every eight years or so in adulthood. In 2018, a study of thousands of naked mole-rats found that their risk of dying did not increase with age. An old naked mole-rat was no more likely to die than a young one.

[visual: microscope cells]
They also very rarely develop cancer. Scientists have proposed several reasons, including a special form of a substance called hyaluronan, which seems to make their tissues more resistant to tumours.

[visual: breathing air concept]
And they have one more trick. In 2017, researchers found that naked mole-rats can survive about 18 minutes without any oxygen at all. During that time, their bodies switch to burning a different sugar, fructose, in a way that is normally seen in plants. A human brain without oxygen begins to suffer serious damage within minutes.

# The trees that remember the pyramids

[visual: ancient pine tree mountain]
Before we finish, let's leave the animal kingdom for a moment. High in the White Mountains of California grow the Great Basin bristlecone pines. They're twisted, battered by wind and partly dead, with only thin strips of living bark.

[visual: tree trunk twisted]
One of them, known as Methuselah, has been estimated by counting tree rings at more than 4,800 years old. It was already a few centuries old when the Great Pyramid of Giza was built. Its exact location is kept secret to protect it.

[visual: tree rings ancient]
Bristlecone pines live so long partly because they grow incredibly slowly, in a harsh place where few pests or competitors can survive. Their wood is dense and resinous, resistant to rot and insects. And they can let parts of themselves die, while keeping a small strip of living tissue going. They survive by sacrificing what they don't need.

# The lobster myth

[visual: lobster underwater]
And now, a quick myth to bust. You may have read online that lobsters are immortal. The story goes that lobsters keep producing an enzyme called telomerase, which helps repair the ends of chromosomes, so they don't age and just keep growing forever.

[visual: lobster shell close up]
Lobsters do produce telomerase in their tissues, and they do keep growing throughout their lives. But they are not immortal. To grow, a lobster has to moult, shed its entire shell and grow a new one. The bigger it gets, the more energy each moult costs. Eventually, very large lobsters can die from exhaustion during moulting, or they stop moulting, and their old shells become damaged and infected. Long-lived, yes. Immortal, no.

# What it means for us

[visual: elderly hands holding]
So, what do these creatures tell us about our own lives?

[visual: dna helix spinning]
First, that aging is not a fixed law of the universe. Different species age at radically different speeds, and some barely seem to age at all. That means aging is a biological process, and biological processes can, at least in principle, be studied, understood, and perhaps slowed down.

[visual: scientist lab research]
Second, there's a recurring theme in almost every story we've seen today: maintenance. The immortal jellyfish has a powerful toolkit for repairing DNA and reprogramming cells. The Greenland shark carries extra DNA repair genes. Tardigrades protect their DNA with a special protein. Naked mole-rats resist cancer and protect their cells. Living long is not about being strong. It's about repairing damage well.

[visual: research medicine pills]
Researchers are already studying these animals for ideas. Proteins like Dsup are being tested to protect cells from radiation, which could matter for cancer treatment or for astronauts on long missions. The biology of freezing frogs could help preserve organs for transplant. And the strange metabolism of the naked mole-rat could inspire ways to protect the brain during a stroke or heart attack.

[visual: sunset ocean calm]
But maybe there's a quieter lesson too. Many of these creatures don't survive by fighting harder. They survive by slowing down, by waiting, by pausing when the world becomes hostile and waking up when it's safe again. A tardigrade drying in the sun. A frog frozen under the snow. A shark gliding slowly through the dark for four centuries.

So here's my question for you: if you could have one of these abilities, which would you choose? Turning young again like the jellyfish, pausing time like the tardigrade, or living four hundred years like the Greenland shark? Tell me in the comments.
