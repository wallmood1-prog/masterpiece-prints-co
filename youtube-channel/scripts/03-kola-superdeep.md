# A hole in the Arctic

[visual: arctic tundra landscape]
In the far north of Russia, near the border with Norway, there's a patch of abandoned land on the Kola Peninsula. Rusting buildings. Broken windows. Concrete covered in graffiti. And in the middle of it all, on the ground, there's a small, heavy metal cap, bolted shut. It's about the size of a dinner plate.

[visual: rusty metal abandoned]
You could walk past it without noticing. But under that cap is the deepest hole humans have ever drilled into the Earth. It goes down more than twelve kilometres. If you dropped a stone into it, and there was nothing to stop it, it would fall for longer than you'd think possible.

[visual: drilling rig night]
This is the Kola Superdeep Borehole. For almost twenty years, scientists drilled into the crust of our planet, deeper than anyone had ever gone before. They expected to find certain things. Instead, they found surprises that rewrote textbooks. And in the end, they had to stop, not because they ran out of money or ambition, but because the Earth itself refused to let them go further.

[visual: earth globe space]
Today we're going down that hole. We'll see what they found, why they couldn't continue, and how a hole twenty-three centimetres wide became the subject of one of the strangest legends of the twentieth century: the well to hell.

# The race to the inside

[visual: cold war vintage footage]
To understand why the Soviet Union drilled this hole, we have to go back to the Cold War. In the 1950s and 1960s, the United States and the Soviet Union competed in everything: weapons, sport, technology, and of course space. Everybody remembers the race to the Moon. Fewer people know there was also a race in the opposite direction: down.

[visual: ocean waves ship]
In the late 1950s, American scientists launched Project Mohole. The goal was to drill through the Earth's crust and reach the boundary below it, called the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or Moho for short. That's the line where the crust ends and the mantle begins. The Americans chose to drill from a ship in the ocean, because the oceanic crust is much thinner than continental crust.

[visual: drilling ship sea]
In 1961, off the coast of Mexico, they drilled about 180 metres into the sea floor. It was an engineering milestone. But the project ran into budget problems and politics, and the US Congress cancelled it in 1966.

[visual: snow forest russia]
The Soviet Union chose a different strategy. Instead of the thin ocean crust, they would drill straight down into the continent, into some of the oldest rocks on the planet. The Kola Peninsula sits on the Baltic Shield, a block of crust that is billions of years old. If you wanted to read the deep history of the Earth, this was the place to start.

# Drilling begins

[visual: drill bit machinery]
On the 24th of May, 1970, drilling began. The project was called SG-3, short for "superdeep" in Russian. And the first thing to understand is how small the hole was. Its diameter was only about twenty-three centimetres, roughly nine inches. You couldn't climb into it. You couldn't even fit your head into it.

[visual: drilling pipe turning]
And the drilling technique itself was clever. With normal drilling, the whole pipe rotates from the surface, turning the drill bit at the bottom. But when your pipe is kilometres long, rotating all that steel becomes a nightmare. It twists, it vibrates, it breaks. So the Soviet engineers used turbines at the bottom of the hole. Drilling fluid, a kind of mud, was pumped down the pipe, and the flow spun a turbine that turned the drill bit. The pipe itself could stay still while only the bottom part rotated.

[visual: rocks core samples]
As they drilled, they pulled up cores, cylinders of rock from deep below. Each core was a message from the inside of the planet. And very quickly, the messages started to contradict what scientists expected.

# The first surprise: no basalt

[visual: geology layers rock]
Before drilling, geologists had a theory based on how earthquake waves travel through the Earth. Seismic waves seemed to change speed at a depth of around seven kilometres under the Baltic Shield. Most scientists interpreted this as the boundary between granite above and a different type of rock below, basalt.

[visual: granite rock texture]
So when the drill passed seven kilometres, everyone expected to hit basalt. It didn't. There was no basalt. The rock was still granite-like, but changed. It was fractured and full of water. The change in the seismic signal had been caused by the rock being cracked and transformed by heat and pressure, not by a new layer. One of the main assumptions of how continental crust was structured had just been challenged.

# The second surprise: water

[visual: water drops close up]
And then there was the water itself. At depths of several kilometres, the scientists found water circulating in the cracks of the rock. That was shocking, because at that depth there should be no way for surface water to seep down through solid rock.

[visual: crystal minerals]
The explanation was even stranger. The water wasn't from the surface. It had been created down there. The hydrogen and oxygen atoms had been squeezed out of minerals by the enormous pressure, and then trapped under a layer of impermeable rock, unable to escape. The Earth was, in a sense, making its own water deep below our feet.

# The third surprise: fossils

[visual: microscope view plankton]
The third surprise was alive. Or rather, it had been alive, a very long time ago. At a depth of around six kilometres, in rock that was around two billion years old, the scientists found microscopic fossils of plankton. Tiny single-celled organisms.

[visual: fossils macro]
Scientists reported over twenty species of these microfossils. What was really surprising was how well preserved they were. They weren't encased in limestone or silica as fossils normally are. They were preserved in carbon and nitrogen compounds, and they had survived the immense heat and pressure deep in the crust. Life, it turned out, could leave its signature in places nobody expected.

# The fourth surprise: the boiling mud

[visual: bubbling mud geyser]
And then there was the hydrogen. As the drilling mud came back up from the depths, it was bubbling, almost as if it were boiling. But it wasn't boiling. It was saturated with hydrogen gas, coming from deep within the rock. Scientists were surprised by how much of it there was. Today, interest in natural hydrogen as a clean energy source has exploded, and deep drilling sites around the world are being studied again with this in mind.

# The heat

[visual: lava molten rock]
But the biggest surprise was the one that ultimately ended the project: the heat.

[visual: thermometer heat]
The deeper you go into the Earth, the hotter it gets. Everybody knew that. Based on their models, the Soviet scientists expected that at around fifteen kilometres, their target depth, the temperature would be around 100 degrees Celsius. Hot, but manageable for drilling equipment.

[visual: fire heat waves]
The reality was very different. As they went deeper, the temperature climbed much faster than predicted. At just over twelve kilometres, it reached about 180 degrees Celsius, around 356 degrees Fahrenheit.

[visual: melting plastic slow]
At that temperature and pressure, the rock started to behave strangely. It wasn't fully solid anymore. It was more like plastic, or very thick toothpaste. When the drill was pulled out to replace the bit, the walls of the hole would slowly close in and flow back into the space. Every time they pulled the drill up, the Earth was trying to heal the wound.

[visual: broken metal machinery]
The equipment struggled. Drill bits wore out quickly. Pipes broke. And it took hours and hours just to pull the drill string up from such depth to change a bit, and then lower it back down again.

# The record

[visual: measuring tape depth]
Despite all this, in 1989, the borehole reached a depth of 12,262 metres. That's twelve point two kilometres. About forty thousand feet straight down.

[visual: mount everest mountain]
To put that in perspective: Mount Everest is about 8,849 metres tall. If you could put Everest at the bottom of the Kola hole, its peak would still be more than three kilometres below the surface.

[visual: deep ocean dark]
The deepest point of the ocean, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, is about 11 kilometres deep. The Kola borehole goes deeper than that, through solid rock.

[visual: earth core diagram]
And yet. The Earth's radius is about 6,371 kilometres. The Kola Superdeep Borehole, the greatest drilling achievement in human history, went through about 0.2 percent of the way to the centre. If the Earth were an apple, we haven't even made it through the skin. The continental crust in that region is estimated to be around 35 to 40 kilometres thick. They didn't even get a third of the way through it.

# Why they stopped

[visual: abandoned building snow]
The target had been fifteen kilometres. They never reached it. In 1992, drilling stopped. The heat made going deeper technically impossible with the technology of the time.

[visual: soviet union flag history]
And the world above had changed too. The Soviet Union collapsed at the end of 1991. Funding dried up. Scientific work at the site continued for some years, but eventually, the project was closed. The site was abandoned, and the hole was sealed with that small metal cap, welded shut.

[visual: graffiti ruins]
Today the buildings are ruins, visited by urban explorers and the occasional curious tourist. It's one of the most important scientific sites of the twentieth century, and it looks like a forgotten factory.

# The well to hell

[visual: dark fire flames]
But no story about the Kola borehole is complete without the legend. Around 1989, a story started spreading. It claimed that a team of scientists, drilling a superdeep well somewhere in Siberia, had broken into a huge cavity at a depth of around fourteen kilometres. The temperature, the story said, was over 1,000 degrees Celsius.

[visual: microphone recording old]
They lowered a heat-resistant microphone into the hole to listen. And what they heard, according to the story, were screams. Thousands of human voices, screaming in pain. The scientists, terrified, fled. Some versions add that a creature with bat wings flew out of the hole.

[visual: old radio broadcast]
The story spread through Finnish newspapers, then through Christian radio and television broadcasts in the United States, as supposed proof of the existence of hell. Later, it lived again on the internet, with an audio recording of screams attached to it.

[visual: horror movie sound studio]
Of course, none of it was real. The recording was eventually traced back to a manipulated soundtrack, reportedly taken from a horror film. The depths, the temperatures and the details didn't match anything about the real Kola project. There was no microphone. There were no screams. There were no bats. The real temperature at the bottom, as we've seen, was around 180 degrees, not a thousand.

[visual: question mark mystery]
But the legend says something interesting about us. Humans have always imagined the underworld as a place of fire and torment. And when real scientists actually went deep into the Earth, the imagination of millions filled the hole with demons, instead of microfossils and hydrogen bubbles. The truth was stranger and more beautiful than the myth, but less dramatic for the radio.

# Has anyone gone deeper

[visual: offshore oil rig]
So, has anyone beaten the Kola record? The answer is: yes and no.

[visual: oil drilling ocean]
Some oil and gas wells are actually longer than the Kola hole. A well in the Al Shaheen oil field in Qatar, drilled in 2008, reached a length of about 12,289 metres. Wells off Sakhalin Island in Russia have reached more than 12 kilometres as well. But these wells go mostly sideways, curving through oil-bearing rock layers. Their length is impressive, but they don't go nearly as deep vertically.

[visual: german forest drilling]
In terms of pure vertical depth, the Kola borehole still holds the record. The German KTB project in Bavaria, in the late 1980s and early 1990s, reached around 9.1 kilometres, and found temperatures above 260 degrees Celsius, even hotter than Kola at a shallower depth. More recently, China has drilled ultra-deep wells in the Tarim Basin, with targets of more than eleven kilometres, partly for science and partly to find oil and gas.

[visual: deep sea drilling ship]
And the dream of reaching the mantle is still alive. The Japanese drilling ship Chikyu was designed to drill through the thin oceanic crust into the mantle. In 2023, an international expedition aboard another drilling ship, the JOIDES Resolution, recovered a long core of mantle rock from the floor of the Atlantic Ocean, in a place where the mantle is pushed up close to the surface. We're getting closer to holding the inside of our planet in our hands.

# Why it matters

[visual: geothermal power plant]
So, was the Kola borehole worth it? Absolutely. It showed us that the crust is far more complex, wet, hot and alive with chemistry than we thought. It taught us that our models, built from earthquake waves and lab experiments, can be wrong in important ways.

[visual: volcano steam]
And the questions it raised are more relevant than ever. Deep heat is a potential source of clean energy. Geothermal projects around the world are trying to drill deeper and hotter, using new technologies, some even borrowing techniques from oil drilling and from experimental methods like plasma and millimetre-wave drilling. The temperatures that stopped the Soviet drill might one day power our cities.

[visual: hydrogen energy concept]
Natural hydrogen, like the gas that made the Kola mud bubble, is now being studied as a possible energy source. And the deep biosphere, the microbes living kilometres underground, has become one of the most exciting fields in biology.

# The cap in the ground

[visual: arctic landscape sunset]
Let's go back to that small metal cap in the Arctic. It covers a hole twenty-three centimetres wide and more than twelve kilometres deep. It represents twenty years of work, thousands of drill bits, countless broken pipes and a generation of scientists who wanted to know what is beneath our feet.

[visual: stars night sky]
We've sent probes beyond the edge of our Solar System. Voyager 1 is more than twenty-four billion kilometres away. And yet, the deepest we have ever gone into our own planet is just twelve kilometres. The inside of the Earth remains one of the least explored places we know.
