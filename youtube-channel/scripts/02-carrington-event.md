# Fire on the wires

[visual: old telegraph machine]
On the night of the 1st of September, 1859, a telegraph operator in the United States was sitting at his desk when sparks began jumping from his equipment. Not from a storm outside. There was no lightning. The sparks came from the wires themselves.

[visual: sparks electricity dark]
Across Europe and North America, the same thing was happening. In some telegraph offices, operators received electric shocks. Paper strips used to record messages caught fire. Lines went silent, or started tapping out gibberish nobody had sent.

[visual: aurora borealis night sky]
And above all of this, the sky was on fire. Auroras, the northern lights, which normally dance near the Arctic, were blazing in red, green and purple over places that almost never see them. People saw them in the Caribbean. In the Rocky Mountains, the sky was reportedly so bright that some gold miners woke up in the middle of the night and started making breakfast, thinking it was morning.

[visual: sun surface close up]
Nobody on Earth understood what was happening. But one man, a few hours earlier, had seen the cause with his own eyes. He just didn't know it yet.

[visual: earth from space]
This is the story of the Carrington Event, the most powerful solar storm in recorded history. It's the story of what the Sun did to a world connected by a few telegraph wires. And it's also the story of what the same storm could do to our world today, a world that runs on electricity, satellites and the internet. Because scientists are clear about one thing: it is not a question of if it will happen again. It's a question of when.

# The man who watched the Sun

[visual: english countryside manor]
Let's start a few hours before the sparks. It's late morning on the 1st of September, 1859, in Redhill, a town just south of London. A 33-year-old astronomer named Richard Carrington is in his private observatory, doing what he does every clear day: drawing the Sun.

[visual: old telescope brass]
Looking directly at the Sun through a telescope would blind you instantly. So Carrington used a safer method. He projected the image of the Sun from his telescope onto a screen, and traced the dark sunspots with a pencil. It was patient, careful work. He was building a record of how sunspots appeared, moved and disappeared.

[visual: bright light flash]
At around a quarter past eleven, while sketching a large group of sunspots, he saw something he had never seen before. Two brilliant patches of white light suddenly appeared inside the sunspot group. They were so bright they outshone the surface of the Sun around them.

[visual: man running hallway]
Carrington later wrote that he was so surprised that he rushed out of the room to find someone to witness it. When he came back, less than a minute later, the light was already fading. Within about five minutes, it was gone.

[visual: old letters writing]
Fortunately, he wasn't the only witness. Another English astronomer, Richard Hodgson, saw the same flash independently from his own observatory. Two observers, two locations, the same event. It was real.

[visual: magnetic compass needle]
And there was a third witness, one that didn't have eyes. At the Kew Observatory near London, instruments that measured Earth's magnetic field recorded a sudden jolt at almost exactly the moment Carrington saw the flash. Something had happened on the Sun, and Earth had felt it immediately.

[visual: sun flare animation]
What Carrington saw is what we now call a solar flare: a gigantic explosion on the Sun's surface, releasing an enormous amount of energy. It was the first solar flare ever observed and recorded by humans.

# The cloud that crossed space

[visual: space stars dark]
But the flare itself was just the flash. The real danger was on its way.

[visual: solar eruption plasma]
Big flares are often accompanied by something called a coronal mass ejection, or CME. Imagine the Sun throwing a gigantic cloud of electrically charged gas into space, billions of tonnes of material, carrying its own magnetic field with it. A CME is not light. It doesn't travel at the speed of light. It's matter, and it has to physically cross the 150 million kilometres between the Sun and the Earth.

[visual: clock time passing]
Normally, that journey takes two to four days. The cloud from the Carrington flare made it in about seventeen and a half hours. That's astonishingly fast. One explanation is that an earlier eruption, just a few days before, had cleared the path, like a snowplough clearing a road for the car behind it.

[visual: earth magnetic field]
When the cloud hit Earth, it slammed into our planet's magnetic field, the invisible shield that protects us from most of the Sun's radiation. The magnetic field was squeezed, twisted and shaken. And when a magnetic field changes rapidly near long metal wires, it pushes electric currents through them. That's basic physics, the same principle that makes a power station generator work.

[visual: telegraph poles landscape]
In 1859, the longest metal wires on Earth were telegraph lines, stretching for hundreds of kilometres across continents. And so the storm poured electricity into them. That's where the sparks came from. That's why paper caught fire.

# The telegraph that ran on the sky

[visual: vintage office desk]
And then came one of the strangest moments in the history of technology. Between Boston and Portland, in Maine, two telegraph operators noticed that their lines were behaving oddly. The current from the storm was so strong that they decided to try something. They disconnected the batteries that normally powered the line.

[visual: morse code tapping]
And the telegraph kept working. For about two hours, they sent messages to each other using only the electricity induced by the aurora. They were literally communicating with power from the sky.

[visual: newspaper reading night]
Meanwhile, the auroras lit up the night. In the northeastern United States, people reported that the light was bright enough to read a newspaper by. Some people thought a city was burning on the horizon. Others feared it was the end of the world. Newspapers printed pages of eyewitness accounts in the days that followed.

[visual: red sky night]
The storm faded within a couple of days. Telegraph lines were repaired. Life went on. In 1859, the world's dependence on electricity was tiny. A few broken lines were an inconvenience, not a catastrophe.

[visual: modern city night lights]
Now, imagine the same storm hitting the world of today.

# How do we know how big it was

[visual: tree rings close up]
Before we talk about the modern risk, there's an important question: was the Carrington Event really unique? Or was it just the biggest storm that happened to be recorded?

[visual: ice glacier antarctica]
Scientists have found ways to look further back in time. Extreme solar events leave chemical fingerprints on Earth. When high-energy particles from the Sun hit our atmosphere, they create rare forms of elements, like carbon-14 and beryllium-10. These end up stored in tree rings and in layers of polar ice.

[visual: ancient forest trees]
In 2012, a team of Japanese researchers led by Fusa Miyake discovered a sudden spike of carbon-14 in tree rings dating to the years 774 and 775. The spike was so sharp that it pointed to an extreme burst of radiation hitting the Earth in a very short time. The leading explanation is a solar storm, or rather a solar particle event, much stronger than anything in modern records.

[visual: old manuscript medieval]
Since then, more of these Miyake events have been found, in different centuries and even thousands of years ago. The message is uncomfortable: the Carrington Event was not the worst the Sun can do. It's just the worst we happened to see.

# The storms of the modern age

[visual: power lines snow winter]
And we've already had warnings. On the 13th of March, 1989, a powerful geomagnetic storm hit Earth. In Québec, Canada, the storm induced currents in the power grid. Protective systems tripped one after another, and in about 90 seconds, the entire Hydro-Québec grid collapsed.

[visual: dark city blackout]
Around six million people lost power for about nine hours, in the middle of a Canadian winter. That storm was strong, but far weaker than Carrington.

[visual: satellite orbit earth]
In late October 2003, a series of powerful flares and CMEs, known as the Halloween storms, hit Earth. Satellites were damaged or temporarily disabled. Airlines rerouted flights away from polar routes to protect passengers and crews from radiation and to avoid communication blackouts. In Sweden, the city of Malmö had a short blackout.

[visual: rocket launch night]
And then, in February 2022, the company SpaceX launched 49 new Starlink satellites. A minor geomagnetic storm, nothing dramatic, heated and expanded the upper atmosphere, increasing drag on the satellites, which were still in a low orbit. About 38 of them couldn't recover and fell back to burn up in the atmosphere. A small storm had destroyed tens of millions of dollars of hardware in a few days.

[visual: aurora over city]
Most recently, in May 2024, Earth was hit by the strongest geomagnetic storm in over twenty years. Auroras were seen across Europe and the United States, much further south than usual. Millions of people photographed them with their phones. It was beautiful. But behind the scenes, operators were busy. Some precision GPS systems used by farmers during planting season were disrupted, and satellite operators had to manage the effects on their fleets.

# The near miss of 2012

[visual: sun eruption space]
But the most chilling story is one that most people have never heard of. On the 23rd of July, 2012, the Sun produced an enormous CME. Scientists who analysed it later concluded it was comparable to the Carrington Event, possibly even stronger.

[visual: earth orbit animation]
It crossed Earth's orbit. But Earth wasn't there. Our planet had passed that point in its orbit about nine days earlier. The cloud missed us.

[visual: space probe satellite]
We only know about it in detail because a NASA spacecraft called STEREO-A was in the right place to be hit, and it measured the storm directly. Daniel Baker, a space physicist from the University of Colorado who studied the event, said that if the eruption had happened just a week earlier, Earth would have been in the line of fire. And he added that we would still be picking up the pieces.

[visual: dice probability]
That same year, the physicist Pete Riley published a study estimating that the probability of a Carrington-class storm hitting Earth in the following decade was around 12 percent. Other researchers have since produced different estimates, some lower, some higher. But all of them agree on one thing: it's not science fiction. It's a real, recurring natural hazard, like an earthquake or a hurricane.

# What would happen today

[visual: power transformer substation]
So, what would a Carrington-class storm do to us today? Let's go step by step.

[visual: high voltage lines]
First, the power grid. Long high-voltage transmission lines act like giant antennas for geomagnetically induced currents. Those currents can flow into large transformers, the huge machines that step voltage up and down. They can overheat them and, in the worst case, permanently damage them.

[visual: factory heavy machinery]
And here's the problem. Large power transformers are not something you buy off the shelf. They're often custom-built, weigh hundreds of tonnes and can take a year or more to manufacture and deliver. If many of them failed at the same time, some regions could face very long blackouts.

[visual: satellites space station]
Second, satellites. Thousands of satellites orbit the Earth today. They provide GPS, weather forecasting, communications, television and internet. A major storm can damage their electronics, scramble their signals and increase drag on those in low orbit, as the Starlink loss showed.

[visual: smartphone map navigation]
And GPS is not just about maps on your phone. Banks, stock markets, mobile phone networks and power grids use GPS timing signals to synchronise their systems. A long GPS disruption would ripple through systems you never think about.

[visual: airplane cockpit]
Third, aviation. During major storms, flights over the poles are rerouted, because radio communication can fail and radiation exposure increases at high altitude.

[visual: server room data center]
Fourth, the internet. Most of the internet's long-distance traffic travels through fibre-optic cables, which are not affected by induced currents. But long undersea cables contain electrical components, called repeaters, that boost the signal along the way. Some researchers have raised concerns that a severe storm could affect these powered components. This is still an area of active research, and nobody knows exactly how resilient the system would be.

[visual: money calculator]
In 2013, the insurance market Lloyd's of London and the consultancy AER published a report on what a Carrington-level storm might cost the United States alone. Their estimate ranged from 0.6 to 2.6 trillion dollars, with recovery potentially taking years.

[visual: aurora people watching]
And yet, here's the strange part: for most people on the ground, the storm itself would be harmless. Our atmosphere and magnetic field protect our bodies. You wouldn't feel a thing. You'd just look up and see the most spectacular aurora of your life. The danger is to the technology we've built, not to our skin.

# Are we ready

[visual: scientist monitoring screens]
So, is there anything we can do? The good news is: yes, quite a lot.

[visual: satellite in space sun]
First, we're watching. Spacecraft like NASA and NOAA's DSCOVR sit about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, in the direction of the Sun. When a CME passes them, we get a warning, typically somewhere between fifteen minutes and an hour before it hits. Other observatories watch the Sun constantly and can see eruptions as they happen, giving a rough warning of a day or more.

[visual: control room operators]
Second, grid operators can prepare. With enough warning, they can reduce loads, reconfigure networks and protect the most vulnerable equipment. Some countries have installed devices that block geomagnetically induced currents, and have stockpiled spare transformers.

[visual: space probe sun]
Third, we're studying the Sun closer than ever. NASA's Parker Solar Probe has flown through the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, closer than any human-made object before it. The more we understand how the Sun builds up and releases energy, the better our forecasts will be.

[visual: sun rotating time lapse]
And there's a natural rhythm to watch. The Sun goes through cycles of activity that last about eleven years, from quiet minimum to stormy maximum. In 2024, NASA and NOAA announced that the Sun had reached the maximum period of its current cycle. Big storms are more likely around solar maximum, but they can happen at any point in the cycle.

# Back to Redhill

[visual: sunset over fields]
Let's go back for a moment to Richard Carrington, in his observatory, on a late summer morning in 1859. He saw two points of light on the Sun, and he had the scientific instinct to write down exactly what he saw, and when. That simple act of careful observation made him the first person to connect an explosion on the Sun with chaos on Earth.

[visual: earth night lights from space]
A hundred and sixty years later, we live on a planet wrapped in power lines, cables and satellites, all of it vulnerable to the same star that keeps us alive. The Sun gives us light, warmth and every bit of energy that powers life on Earth. And every once in a while, it reminds us that it's also a gigantic, unpredictable nuclear furnace.

[visual: aurora reflection lake]
The next Carrington Event is coming. Maybe next year, maybe in a century. When it does, the world will see the most beautiful sky in living memory. And whether that night is just a spectacle, or the beginning of a very long blackout, depends on how seriously we take the warning that a quiet astronomer gave us in 1859.
